THE ALIBI PLAYBOOK

You both know the word. Just not the same one.

Published by Imposter Play · How this guide is made

In Classic mode, an imposter knows they are bluffing. In Undercover, they may be as confident as everyone else. Their card shows a related but different word, and does not identify their role.

One group, two versions of reality

Most players might see “pancake.” The undercover player sees “waffle.” Both can plausibly say “breakfast.” As the clues become more specific, someone’s story stops fitting. The challenge is to notice the difference without blurting out your own word.

How to start an Undercover round

  1. Add the players and choose Undercover in the setup.
  2. Select your topics. Each built-in word includes a related alternative.
  3. Pass the phone and privately check the word on each card.
  4. Give clues, discuss, then choose your suspects as a group.

Do not announce your word or ask other players whether they got the same one. Role cards are intentionally visually identical in this mode. The final reveal names the imposters and shows both words.

What makes a fair pair?

A good pair shares a setting, purpose or shape, while leaving room for a revealing difference. “Train” and “tram” overlap naturally. “Train” and “strawberry” turn the game into a quick accident. Custom entries need an alternative in the third column to appear in Undercover rounds.

Which mode should you choose?

Pick Classic when introducing new players: roles are clear and imposters know their task. Pick Undercover when the group is comfortable giving indirect clues. It creates a different kind of suspicion, because a misleading clue can be completely sincere.

The same final accusation rule applies to both modes: civilians must identify every imposter to win the round.

Enough theory. Who’s bluffing?
